## 2. Your Vision

### How will you Build Better Futures? — 175 words maximum

Klinikos is building a governed operating layer for outpatient healthcare. Clinics already use EHRs, practice-management systems, payer portals, labs, imaging systems, phone, fax, email and spreadsheets, yet consequential work still disappears between them. A result can arrive without review, a referral can be ordered without closure, a patient can be booked without being ready, and a completed service can stall before documentation, coding, authorization or payment.

Klinikos is designed around one operating question: what needs to happen next? It combines task-first clinic operations, a converged clinical visit experience, longitudinal Clinical Change, revenue-integrity workflow and Zumi, an AI intelligence layer that can summarize, compare and prepare work while deterministic systems retain authority over permissions, signatures, credentials, payments and other consequential state.

Our near-term objective is to prove one measurable outpatient workflow improvement without requiring an EHR replacement. Longer term, the same governed identity, evidence and workflow substrate can extend into healthcare capacity through Grid and workforce development through Klinikos EDU.

### Describe how it will make a difference to people's lives — 150–200 words

Healthcare technology often adds another place for people to look rather than reducing the work required to coordinate care. Patients feel the downstream effects when follow-up is late, forms are missing, referrals disappear, results are not closed, or administrative work delays the next step. Clinicians and staff experience the same fragmentation as cognitive load, duplicated work, interruptions and time spent reconstructing what already happened.

Klinikos aims to make unfinished work visible and owned. Before a visit, the system can organize what changed and what remains incomplete. During a visit, staff handoff and longitudinal evidence can reduce reconstruction. Afterward, the same workflow can preserve what requires review, documentation, coding, authorization, follow-up or payment attention.

The goal is not to replace professional judgment with AI. Zumi may explain and prepare, while regulated or consequential authority remains with authorized people and deterministic systems. If Klinikos works as intended, patients should experience fewer dropped next steps and healthcare teams should spend more attention on meaningful work instead of chasing fragmented workflow state.

## 3. Desirability

### Clinical problem/challenge — 150–200 words

Outpatient healthcare workflows are fragmented across systems that each hold part of the truth. EHRs may document care, practice systems manage scheduling, payer portals manage authorization, external systems return results, and staff coordinate exceptions through phone, fax, email, spreadsheets and memory. The clinical challenge is that the team often has to reconstruct workflow state manually at exactly the moment attention is scarce.

For a clinician, that can mean rebuilding what changed since a prior visit, separating staff-captured information from provider-reviewed evidence, locating new or corrected results, and identifying what still requires clinical authority. For staff, it can mean discovering too late that intake, follow-up, referral, authorization or documentation work was never completed.

Klinikos treats unfinished work and longitudinal change as first-class evidence. The system is designed to preserve who did what, when it happened, what evidence supports the state, and what action remains, while avoiding unsupported inferences such as treating omission as resolution or a submitted request as completed care.

### Patient/caregiver benefit — 150–200 words

For patients and caregivers, the value is continuity rather than another portal feature. A healthcare journey contains many transitions where important work can become invisible: preparing for a visit, completing forms, obtaining authorization, receiving and reviewing results, arranging referrals, coordinating follow-up, and understanding what happens next.

Klinikos is designed to reduce those dropped transitions by giving the care team a clearer operating picture of what is complete, what changed, what is blocked, and who owns the next step. A patient should not need to understand the clinic's internal software architecture to receive coordinated care.

The system also separates assistance from authority. AI can help organize information or prepare work, but it does not independently diagnose, prescribe, sign clinical documentation, establish credential truth, release records, or claim a result was reviewed when no governed evidence supports that state.

The intended patient benefit is practical: fewer missed next steps, clearer follow-up, less avoidable repetition, and a care team that can spend less effort searching for workflow state and more effort acting on it.

### Usability or human-factors benefits — 150–200 words

Klinikos is designed around reducing reconstruction and navigation burden. Traditional healthcare software often exposes its internal modules to the user: scheduling lives in one place, results in another, billing elsewhere, and staff must learn how the software is organized before they can complete the work.

Our design principle is the opposite: the complexity belongs to Klinikos, not to the person using Klinikos. The authenticated experience asks what needs to happen next and presents information in the shape of the decision it supports. Current Visit converges patient context, what changed, staff handoff, clinical work, orders/results, documentation/coding and visit closure into one workstage rather than forcing clinicians through disconnected modules.

Longitudinal information is structured as initial to previous to today, allowing change to be inspected instead of reconstructed from a stack of notes. Zumi can explain context in plain language but remains bounded by authorization and evidence. The intended human-factors benefit is fewer context switches, less memorization, clearer ownership, and more attention available for patient care and consequential decisions.

## 4. Feasibility

Development stage — 20 words maximum:

Working software platform with implemented core workflows; early commercialization and production hardening underway; external clinical validation still expanding.

### Clinical evidence — 150–200 words

Klinikos is early, so we will not claim clinical outcome evidence that has not been measured. Our current evidence is problem and workflow validation rather than a completed clinical trial.

The product has been repeatedly revised based on direct healthcare operations exposure and professional clinician and healthcare-IT feedback. That feedback has materially changed the architecture: Current Visit was converged around the actual clinical sequence; MA/LPN/RN work is separated and attributed; longitudinal Clinical Change uses evidence-backed comparison rather than generative invention; body-map history is append-only and versioned; labs and imaging preserve source and review state; coding and revenue workflows distinguish stored internal status from external acknowledgment; and Zumi's authority is explicitly bounded.

These changes are meaningful feasibility evidence because they show that domain feedback can alter the system rather than merely validate a predetermined design. The next evidence step is controlled workflow evaluation using synthetic or non-PHI cases and paid no-PHI operational proofs, followed by appropriate real-world clinical validation only after required privacy, security, organizational and research approvals exist.

### Technical support — 150–200 words

Klinikos already has a substantial working software and governance foundation rather than only a concept. The platform includes tenant-scoped authorization, patient and encounter workflows, Current Visit, versioned BodyMap evidence, deterministic longitudinal comparison, labs and imaging evidence lifecycles, task and workflow systems, patient access, billing and revenue-integrity logic, Grid resource architecture, EDU workflows, audit boundaries and governed Zumi orchestration.

A key technical principle is separating probabilistic interpretation from authoritative state. Current Klinikos rules prevent a model from turning booking into fulfillment, a redirect into payment, training into licensure, an uploaded credential into verification, or model prose into clinical signature. Clinical Change similarly requires provenance-supported evidence and does not treat an omitted finding as resolution.

The remaining work is not presented as complete. Production deployment, PHI readiness, external vendor rails and enterprise assurance each have separate evidence gates. Implemented code, deployed runtime, external connectivity and regulated authority are tracked as different facts rather than collapsed into one maturity claim.

### Business model — 150–200 words

Klinikos' initial customers are outpatient healthcare organizations: independent practices, specialty clinics and multi-location groups where important operational work is fragmented across EHRs, practice systems, payer/vendor portals and manual follow-up.

The commercial sequence starts with a low-risk paid operating analysis that identifies workflow fragmentation without requiring PHI. That can progress to an implementation blueprint or paid proof sprint focused on one measurable workflow, then to recurring Klinikos software once value is demonstrated. Current approved commercial anchors include a $500 Clinic Operating Analysis, $1,500 Implementation Blueprint, Founding Clinic Implementation from $8,000, and recurring Core, Growth and Scale software tiers.

The first business does not depend on national marketplace effects. Clinics can buy analysis, implementation and software based on direct operational value. Longer term, the same customers can create additional distribution and economic activity through Grid healthcare capacity and resource matching, Klinikos EDU workforce relationships, enterprise integrations and governed operational intelligence. Those expansion layers are upside rather than assumptions required for the initial business to stand on its own.

### How will the business make a profit? — 150–200 words

The near-term profit model is recurring software plus disciplined implementation and services. Klinikos is founder-led and intentionally capital efficient: core product development, workflow architecture and early commercialization have been built without a large fixed payroll, and expensive external infrastructure is activated only when a real customer workflow requires it.

A clinic can initially generate one-time analysis and implementation revenue, then recurring subscription revenue. At current approved pricing, recurring software ranges from $995 to $3,995 per month before custom enterprise terms. The business can improve gross margin over time by making onboarding, migration, configuration, support and Zumi-assisted workflow setup increasingly repeatable.

The company also intends to consolidate fragmented workflow and vendor spend where it can create customer value and margin without recreating regulated infrastructure irrationally. External labs, payers, clearinghouses, payment networks and other authoritative rails can remain integrations while Klinikos owns the workflow and evidence around them.

Profitability therefore does not require monetizing every future Grid or network interaction. Those capabilities become additional economic engines only after customer demand, legal structure and liquidity justify them.

### Competitor analysis — 150–200 words

Klinikos competes less with one company than with three categories. First are EHR and practice-management platforms such as athenahealth, eClinicalWorks, Tebra and larger enterprise systems. They remain important systems of record, but healthcare organizations still coordinate work across multiple external systems and manual processes. Second are AI point solutions such as documentation assistants and workflow copilots that improve one task but generally do not own end-to-end evidence, authority and completion state. Third is the status quo: phone, fax, email, spreadsheets, portals and staff memory.

Klinikos does not need a clinic to replace its EHR on day one. It enters where unfinished work has measurable operational or financial cost and coordinates workflow around existing systems.

Its differentiation is the governed operating substrate: identity, authorization, clinical context, evidence provenance, longitudinal change, task state, financial truth and explicit human/AI authority boundaries. Models can help explain and prepare work, while the system preserves authoritative state and responsibility.
